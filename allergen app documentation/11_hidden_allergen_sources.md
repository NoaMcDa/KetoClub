# 11 — Hidden allergen sources in restaurant dishes (sourced desk research)

> **Status: desk research, not clinical review.** This document was compiled
> on 2026-10-11 from published pages of allergy charities, regulators and
> clinical institutions. No dietitian, allergist or lawyer has reviewed it.
> It is the *sourced basis* for the bilingual allergen vocabulary in
> `10_epic_allergens.md`; it is not medical advice and must not be shown to
> users as such.
>
> **How the sources were read.** Every direct page fetch from this
> environment was refused by the egress policy (403 on `foodallergy.org`,
> `fda.gov`, `eur-lex.europa.eu`, `food.gov.uk`, `coeliac.org.uk`, `gov.il`
> and every other host tried). Every finding below therefore comes from
> **search-engine result summaries and snippets of the cited pages**, not
> from reading the pages in full. The URLs are real result URLs; the
> wording attributed to a source is the snippet's wording and may be
> paraphrased. **Before a vocabulary entry ships, someone with a browser
> should open the cited page and confirm the claim.** Where a snippet did
> not cover a point, the entry says so rather than inventing a source.

## 0. How to read the entries

Each restriction has five parts, matching what the vocabulary needs:

1. **Direct trigger words** — ingredient names and dish-level synonyms a
   menu uses. These are the high-confidence matches.
2. **Hidden sources in restaurant cooking** — sauces, marinades,
   dressings, thickeners, breading, stocks, "natural flavour", E-numbers.
   Each carries a confidence note:
   - **documented common** — a regulator, charity or clinic names it as a
     common or typical hidden source;
   - **possible** — a source names it as *sometimes* present, or only one
     weaker source names it, or it is inferred from a documented sibling.
3. **Guard phrases** that cancel a trigger. **For a life-threatening
   allergy a guard lowers certainty but never clears it fully**: the
   kitchen may still cross-contact, the substitute may itself contain the
   allergen (a "non-dairy" creamer with casein, a "nut-free" pesto with
   pine nuts), and no source reviewed here treats a menu phrase as proof.
   The epic's rule is: a guard turns a red match into a yellow "ask the
   waiter" match, never into green.
4. **Cross-contact risks in kitchens** documented by the sources.
5. **Known confusions** the sources warn about.

Hebrew terms are given **only where a source gives them** — in practice
the Israeli Ministry of Health's allergen-labelling guide (§17.3), which
carries the Annex II category names in Hebrew. Dish-level Hebrew menu
vocabulary (טחינה, חלבה, בורקס …) belongs to
`12_israeli_and_us_menu_lexicon.md`; the three Hebrew words that appear
below came from the task brief, not from a cited source, and are marked.

Cross-cutting findings the vocabulary should encode once, not per
allergen:

- **Cooking does not remove an allergen.** FDA: "Cooking does not reduce
  or eliminate the chances of a person with a food allergy having a
  reaction" ([FDA CPG 555.250](https://www.fda.gov/media/168000/download)).
  Anaphylaxis UK says the same of sesame and mustard specifically (§11, §13).
- **Fried food is the highest-risk category on any menu.** FARE: "Both
  the grill and the frying oil are ripe for cross-contact"
  ([FARE, Dining Out](https://www.foodallergy.org/resources/dining-out));
  FDA research found shrimp protein in fries cooked in oil previously used
  for shrimp ([FDA CPG 555.250](https://www.fda.gov/media/168000/download));
  CDC: fryer oil "can contain protein from previously fried foods"
  ([CDC MMWR 2017](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/66/wr/mm6615a2.htm)).
- **Sauces are where restaurant reactions hide.** A FARE-hosted registry
  study of peanut/tree-nut reactions in restaurants found that in half the
  cases where staff knew the food contained a nut, it was concealed in
  sauces, dressings or egg rolls; Asian restaurants, ice-cream shops and
  bakeries were the most-cited establishments
  ([FARE, restaurant reactions](https://www.foodallergy.org/resources/characteristics-food-allergic-reactions-united-states-restaurants)).
- **"May contain" is voluntary and inconsistent** in the US (FDA: advisory
  statements are voluntary; [FDA FAQ](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-allergensgluten-free-guidance-documents-regulatory-information/frequently-asked-questions-food-allergen-labeling-guidance-industry))
  and in the UK should only follow a risk assessment and must name the
  allergen ("may contain peanuts", not "may contain nuts";
  [FSA PAL checklist](https://www.food.gov.uk/business-guidance/precautionary-allergen-labelling-checklist)).
  FARE: "the absence of an advisory label does not mean a product is safe"
  ([FARE, PAL blog](https://www.foodallergy.org/fare-blog/blog-post-may-contain-food-thought-about-precautionary-allergen-labeling)).
  Israel keeps "עלול להכיל" unregulated under the 2028 reform (§17.3).
- **Where the allergen is not on the local regulated list, nothing on a
  label or menu need name it**: molluscs, mustard, celery, lupin, pea and
  other legumes in the US; pea, lentil, chickpea everywhere; barley and
  rye are not US allergens so "no allergen statement" ≠ gluten-free
  ([CDF, label reading](https://celiac.org/gluten-free-living/gluten-free-foods/label-reading-the-fda/)).

## 1. Peanuts

**Regulated:** US 9, EU 14, Israel (בוטנים).

### 1.1 Direct triggers
peanut, peanuts, peanut butter, peanut flour, peanut oil (cold-pressed /
expelled / extruded / "gourmet"), **arachis oil** (another name for peanut
oil), groundnut, monkey nut, satay / sate, peanut sauce, boiled peanuts,
peanut shoots. Sources: [FARE peanut](https://www.foodallergy.org/living-food-allergy/food-allergy-essentials/common-allergens/peanut),
[Anaphylaxis UK peanut & tree nut](https://anaphylaxis.org.uk/factsheets/peanut-and-tree-nut-allergy/).

### 1.2 Hidden sources in restaurant cooking

| Hidden source | Confidence | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Sauces: chili sauce, hot sauce, pesto, gravy, mole, enchilada sauce, glazes, marinades, salad dressing | documented common | [FARE peanut](https://www.foodallergy.org/living-food-allergy/food-allergy-essentials/common-allergens/peanut) |
| Egg rolls; chili (peanut butter used as a thickener) | documented common | [FARE peanut](https://www.foodallergy.org/living-food-allergy/food-allergy-essentials/common-allergens/peanut) |
| Satay sauce; curries and other Asian dishes; salads and dressings; gluten-free alternatives | documented common | [Anaphylaxis UK](https://anaphylaxis.org.uk/factsheets/peanut-and-tree-nut-allergy/) |
| African, Asian (esp. Chinese, Indian, Indonesian, Thai, Vietnamese) and Mexican cuisine — high cross-contact risk "even when you order a dish without peanuts" | documented common | [FARE peanut](https://www.foodallergy.org/living-food-allergy/food-allergy-essentials/common-allergens/peanut) |
| Desserts bought in from specialty shops without full ingredient lists; ice cream, granola, pancakes, trail mix, candy, pudding, cookies, pies, hot chocolate | documented common | [FARE peanut](https://www.foodallergy.org/living-food-allergy/food-allergy-essentials/common-allergens/peanut) |
| Vegetarian meat substitutes; alternative nut/seed butters (soy-nut, sunflower) made on shared lines | possible | [FARE peanut](https://www.foodallergy.org/living-food-allergy/food-allergy-essentials/common-allergens/peanut) |
| Peanut shoots mistaken for bean sprouts in stir-fries and salads | possible | [Anaphylaxis UK](https://anaphylaxis.org.uk/factsheets/peanut-and-tree-nut-allergy/) |
| Gochujang glaze (2022 recall, undeclared peanut from a supplier) | possible | [FARE peanut](https://www.foodallergy.org/living-food-allergy/food-allergy-essentials/common-allergens/peanut) |
| **Mustard ingredients contaminated with peanut** (UK FSA/FSS alert, Sept 2025: peanut-allergic people told to avoid mustard, mustard seeds, powder and flour) | possible, time-bound | [Allergy UK news](https://www.allergyuk.org/news/peanut-statement-update2/) |

Commercial **highly refined** peanut oil is excluded from the US allergen
definition ([FDA CPG 555.250](https://www.fda.gov/media/168000/download));
FARE says to avoid cold-pressed/expelled/extruded ("gourmet") peanut oil
because it may contain protein. A menu rarely says which, so treat
"peanut oil" as a trigger and "fried in vegetable oil" as unknown.

### 1.3 Guard phrases
"peanut-free", "no peanuts", "made without peanuts". **Not a guard:**
"nut-free" (see 1.5). A guard lowers certainty only: cross-contact in
Asian/African/Mexican kitchens is documented even for dishes without
peanuts (FARE).

### 1.4 Cross-contact
Shared fryers and grills (FDA, CDC, FARE — §0); shared scoops in ice-cream
parlours ([FARE, Dining Out](https://www.foodallergy.org/resources/dining-out));
bakeries and buffets (same); Asian restaurants as the most-reported
reaction site ([FARE registry blog](https://www.foodallergy.org/fare-blog/fare-patient-registry-data-sheds-light-prevention-and-management-food-allergy-reactions)).

### 1.5 Confusions
- **Peanut is a legume, not a tree nut.** "Nut-free" is a tree-nut claim
  and does not rule out pine nuts either
  ([Anaphylaxis UK seeds](https://www.anaphylaxis.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/SEEDSA2.pdf)).
- Peanut-allergic people are often advised to avoid tree nuts because of
  manufacturing cross-contact, and a minority react to other legumes
  (lupin, soy, pea, chickpea, fenugreek, beans, lentils) — §16
  ([FARE tree nut](https://www.foodallergy.org/living-food-allergies/food-allergy-essentials/common-allergens/tree-nut),
  [Anaphylaxis UK legumes](https://www.anaphylaxis.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2022/10/Legume-Factsheet-V10-Final-2025.pdf)).

## 2. Tree nuts

**Regulated:** US 9 (as a group; FDA's named list changed in Jan 2025),
EU 14 (**only eight named nuts**), Israel (אגוזים).

### 2.1 Direct triggers
**EU Annex II names:** almonds, hazelnuts, walnuts, cashews, pecan nuts,
Brazil nuts, pistachio nuts, macadamia / Queensland nuts
([legislation.gov.uk Annex II](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2011/1169/annex/II),
[Reading allergen list](https://www.reading.ac.uk/foodlaw/label/allergens-list.htm)).
**FARE's six most common:** walnut, almond, hazelnut, pecan, cashew,
pistachio; FARE's full list also has chestnut, pine nut, pili nut
([FARE tree nut](https://www.foodallergy.org/living-food-allergies/food-allergy-essentials/common-allergens/tree-nut)).
**FSANZ required names** (named individually since PEAL): almond, Brazil
nut, cashew, hazelnut, macadamia, pecan, pistachio, pine nut, walnut
([FSANZ business](https://www.foodstandards.gov.au/business/labelling/allergen-labelling)).
Derived words: marzipan / almond paste, praline, pesto, nut milks, nut
oils, nut extracts (almond, walnut), frangipane (inferred from almond
paste — possible), gianduja (inferred from hazelnut — possible).

### 2.2 Hidden sources

| Hidden source | Confidence | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Marzipan / almond paste; praline; pesto (pine nuts or walnuts) | documented common | [FARE tree nut](https://www.foodallergy.org/living-food-allergies/food-allergy-essentials/common-allergens/tree-nut), [Anaphylaxis UK](https://anaphylaxis.org.uk/factsheets/peanut-and-tree-nut-allergy/) |
| Satay sauce, salads and dressings, gluten-free alternatives; Thai, Indian, Chinese cuisine | documented common | [Anaphylaxis UK](https://anaphylaxis.org.uk/factsheets/peanut-and-tree-nut-allergy/) |
| Nut milks, nut oils, natural nut extracts (almond, walnut flavouring) | documented common | [FARE tree nut](https://www.foodallergy.org/living-food-allergies/food-allergy-essentials/common-allergens/tree-nut) |
| Ice-cream parlours (shared scoops), bakeries (unpackaged goods outside labelling law) | documented common | [FARE, Dining Out](https://www.foodallergy.org/resources/dining-out) |
| Cashew ↔ pistachio: "a significant number" of cashew-allergic people also react to pistachio | documented | [Anaphylaxis UK](https://anaphylaxis.org.uk/factsheets/peanut-and-tree-nut-allergy/) |

### 2.3 Guard phrases
"nut-free" (tree nuts only — does not cover peanut or, per Anaphylaxis
UK, pine nuts), "no nuts", "made without nuts". **"Coconut milk" is not a
tree-nut trigger** (2.5). As with peanut, a guard only lowers certainty
in a bakery, ice-cream or Asian kitchen.

### 2.4 Cross-contact
As §1.4. FARE additionally advises peanut avoidance for tree-nut-allergic
people because of processing cross-contact.

### 2.5 Confusions
- **Coconut:** FARE — "coconut has typically not been restricted in the
  diets of people with a tree nut allergy"; FDA removed coconut from its
  Tree Nut List in the 5th edition of the allergen FAQ (6 Jan 2025) along
  with beech nut, butternut, chestnut, chinquapin, cola nut, ginkgo,
  hickory, palm nut, pili nut, shea nut and lichee nut; old "Contains:
  tree nuts (coconut)" labels may persist for years
  ([FDA FAQ](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-allergensgluten-free-guidance-documents-regulatory-information/frequently-asked-questions-food-allergen-labeling-guidance-industry),
  [FARE tree nut](https://www.foodallergy.org/living-food-allergies/food-allergy-essentials/common-allergens/tree-nut)).
  Coconut was never on the EU list.
- **Nutmeg and chestnut:** Anaphylaxis UK — "coconut, nutmeg and chestnut
  are not tree nuts" and allergy to them is unusual
  ([Anaphylaxis UK](https://anaphylaxis.org.uk/factsheets/peanut-and-tree-nut-allergy/)).
  (FARE's list does include chestnut; the EU list does not. Model nutmeg
  as *not* a trigger; model chestnut as a low-confidence trigger with a
  jurisdiction note.)
- **"Nut" words that are not nuts** (butternut squash, water chestnut,
  nutmeg, coconut): the matcher must not fire on the substring "nut".
- **Pine nut** is a tree nut for FARE and FSANZ but is not one of the
  eight EU-named nuts, so an EU/Israeli label need not emphasise it.

## 3. Milk (allergy)

**Regulated:** US 9, EU 14 "Milk and products thereof (including
lactose)", Israel (חלב).

### 3.1 Direct triggers
milk, butter, butter fat/oil, buttermilk, cream, cheese, yogurt, ghee,
casein, caseinates, whey (all forms), lactalbumin, lactoglobulin, lactose,
curds, hydrolysates, custard, pudding, artificial butter flavour, any
"lact-" prefix ([FARE milk](https://www.foodallergy.org/living-food-allergy/food-allergy-essentials/common-allergens/milk),
[Mayo milk allergy](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/milk-allergy/symptoms-causes/syc-20375101)).
Milk from other mammals (goat, sheep) — not covered by the snippets
reviewed; treat as a trigger (EU "milk" is not species-limited) and verify.

### 3.2 Hidden sources

| Hidden source | Confidence | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **Butter on grilled steak** — "many restaurants put butter on grilled steaks to add extra flavor. You can't see the butter after it melts" | documented common | [FARE milk](https://www.foodallergy.org/living-food-allergy/food-allergy-essentials/common-allergens/milk) |
| Deli / luncheon meats and hot dogs (casein as binder); deli slicers shared with cheese | documented common | [FARE milk](https://www.foodallergy.org/living-food-allergy/food-allergy-essentials/common-allergens/milk) |
| "Non-dairy" creamer (caseinate); "nondairy" products generally | documented common | [FARE PAL blog](https://www.foodallergy.org/fare-blog/blog-post-may-contain-food-thought-about-precautionary-allergen-labeling), [Mayo](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/milk-allergy/symptoms-causes/syc-20375101) |
| Canned tuna, sausage (casein) | documented | [Mayo](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/milk-allergy/symptoms-causes/syc-20375101) |
| Baked goods, caramel, chocolate, margarine | documented common | [FARE wheat-free/milk PDF](https://www.foodallergy.org/media/1706/download) |
| Enriched breads (brioche, milk loaf) | documented | [MK NHS low-lactose leaflet](https://www.mkuh.nhs.uk/patient-information-leaflet/low-lactose-diet) |
| "Animal-free" / precision-fermented whey and casein — still milk protein | documented | [FARE animal-free dairy](https://www.foodallergy.org/fare-blog/animal-free-dairy-what-know-and-how-identify-products) |
| Wine fined with casein | possible | [PMC fining agents](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3354224/) |
| Mashed potato, bread brushed with butter, cream-finished sauces, risotto | possible — not covered by the snippets; inferred from the butter-on-steak finding | — |

### 3.3 Guard phrases
"dairy-free", "milk-free", "vegan", "plant-based", "oat milk", "soy
milk", "almond milk", **"coconut milk" (not milk)**. **Weak guards:**
"non-dairy" (can contain casein — FARE, Mayo) and **"lactose-free" (still
contains casein and whey — Allergy UK: "commonly perceived that lactose
free products are suitable for people with a cow's milk allergy but this
is not true"; [Allergy UK adult CMA](https://www.allergyuk.org/resources/adult-cows-milk-allergy/)).**
Hebrew kashrut terms (פרווה) are a lexicon matter for file 12.

### 3.4 Cross-contact
Flattop grill carrying egg, milk and wheat from breakfast into lunch
([FDA CPG 555.250](https://www.fda.gov/media/168000/download)); deli
slicers (FARE); shared butter pots and spreads
([Coeliac UK cooking at home](https://www.coeliac.org.uk/living-with-coeliac-disease/food-and-drink/cooking-at-home/cross-contamination/)
— written for gluten, the mechanism is the same); water alone does not
remove milk residue in food-service cleaning, detergent mostly does
([FSA cleaning review](https://www.food.gov.uk/research/food-hypersensitivity/international-review-of-the-literature-and-guidance-on-food-allergen-cleaning?print=1)).

### 3.5 Confusions
- Milk **allergy** (immune, proteins) vs **lactose intolerance**
  (digestive, sugar) — [FARE comparison](https://www.foodallergy.org/resources/milk-allergy-vs-lactose-intolerance).
  The two need separate vocabularies (§4).
- "Non-dairy" is a regulated US term that *permits* casein (FARE).
- Lactose on a label is not always written as "lactose"
  ([NHS lactose intolerance](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/lactose-intolerance/)).

## 4. Lactose (intolerance)

**Regulated:** not an allergen; the EU/Israeli milk entry says "(including
lactose)". Canada lists gluten and sulphites but not lactose.

### 4.1 Direct triggers
milk, cream, ice cream, soft/fresh cheese, milk-based sauces, custard,
milk chocolate, lactose, whey, milk solids, condensed milk; brioche and
milk loaf ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/lactose-intolerance/),
[MK NHS](https://www.mkuh.nhs.uk/patient-information-leaflet/low-lactose-diet)).

### 4.2 Hidden sources

| Hidden source | Confidence | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Label terms other than "lactose": milk, whey, curds | documented common | [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/lactose-intolerance/) |
| Enriched breads (brioche, milk loaf) — most breads are lactose-free | documented | [MK NHS](https://www.mkuh.nhs.uk/patient-information-leaflet/low-lactose-diet) |
| Warm/melted dairy and plain milk are the forms some people tolerate least | documented | [CUH NHS](https://www.cuh.nhs.uk/patient-information/lactose-intolerance-in-children-and-young-people/) |

### 4.3 Guards — the **distinct** lactose guards
These cancel a *lactose* trigger but **not** a milk-allergy trigger:
- **"lactose-free milk / lactose-free"** — the intended product for
  lactose intolerance ([NHS treatment](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/lactose-intolerance/treatment/)).
- **Hard aged cheese**: cheeses aged over 12 months (mature cheddar,
  emmental, gruyère) "contain only traces of lactose"; parmesan very low —
  lactose leaves with the whey and the rest converts to lactic acid
  ([MK NHS](https://www.mkuh.nhs.uk/patient-information-leaflet/low-lactose-diet),
  [PMC PDO cheeses](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8464992/)).
- **Butter** — listed first in reintroduction guides ([CUH NHS](https://www.cuh.nhs.uk/patient-information/lactose-intolerance-in-children-and-young-people/)).
- **Live-culture yogurt** — tolerated by many ([Mayo lactose](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/lactose-intolerance/diagnosis-treatment/drc-20374238)).
- **Ghee** — no source reviewed gives its lactose content; it appears in
  avoidance lists as a dairy fat. Mark as *possible* guard, verify.
- All dairy-free / vegan guards from §3.3 also apply.

Tolerance is dose-dependent and individual ([Mayo lactose](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/lactose-intolerance/diagnosis-treatment/drc-20374238)),
so the UI should present lactose as a *gradient* (hard cheese ≈ fine,
cream sauce ≈ not), not a binary.

### 4.4 Cross-contact
Not relevant: an intolerance is dose-driven; trace contact is not a
documented concern in any source reviewed.

### 4.5 Confusions
- **"Lactose-free" ≠ "dairy-free" / "milk-free"** — the single most
  important rule: a lactose-free guard must never clear a milk-allergy
  trigger (§3.3).
- Hard cheese is *green* for lactose and *red* for milk allergy.

## 5. Eggs

**Regulated:** US 9, EU 14, Israel (ביצים).

### 5.1 Direct triggers
egg, eggs, egg white/yolk, albumin / albumen, ovalbumin, ovomucoid,
ovomucin, any "ova-/ovo-" word, globulin, lysozyme, livetin, vitellin,
meringue / meringue powder, mayonnaise, aioli, eggnog, surimi, egg
noodles, fresh pasta, hollandaise, carbonara, custard, frittata, omelette,
shakshuka (inferred; [task-brief, not sourced])
([FARE egg](https://www.foodallergy.org/living-food-allergy/food-allergy-essentials/common-allergens/egg),
[Mayo egg](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/egg-allergy/symptoms-causes/syc-20372115),
[Allergy UK egg](https://www.allergyuk.org/resources/egg-allergy-factsheet/)).

### 5.2 Hidden sources

| Hidden source | Confidence | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Mayonnaise and mayonnaise-based sauces (raw egg); hollandaise, carbonara (lightly cooked) | documented common | [Allergy UK egg](https://www.allergyuk.org/resources/egg-allergy-factsheet/) |
| Fresh pasta "usually contains egg"; dried pasta usually egg-free except egg noodles and some lasagne sheets | documented common | [Allergy UK egg](https://www.allergyuk.org/resources/egg-allergy-factsheet/) |
| Pastry glaze / egg wash on pies, sausage rolls, buns — "may or may not contain egg in the dough/pastry, filling or glaze" | documented common | [Allergy UK egg](https://www.allergyuk.org/resources/egg-allergy-factsheet/) |
| Cocktails with egg-white foam; eggnog — "always ask before ordering" | documented common | [Allergy UK egg](https://www.allergyuk.org/resources/egg-allergy-factsheet/) |
| Breaded foods, baked goods, frostings, marshmallows, marzipan, pretzels, puddings, custards, salad dressing | documented common | [Mayo egg](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/egg-allergy/symptoms-causes/syc-20372115) |
| Surimi / imitation crab | documented | [FARE egg](https://www.foodallergy.org/living-food-allergy/food-allergy-essentials/common-allergens/egg) |
| Soft meringue / mallow (lemon meringue pie) — worse than hard baked meringue | documented | [Allergy UK egg](https://www.allergyuk.org/resources/egg-allergy-factsheet/) |
| Traditional Quorn (egg white); vegan Quorn does not | documented | [Allergy UK egg](https://www.allergyuk.org/resources/egg-allergy-factsheet/) |
| Omelettes made fluffier with pancake batter (also a wheat hide) | documented | [CDF dining](https://celiac.org/gluten-free-living/dining-and-social-eating/) |
| Wine fined with egg white / lysozyme | possible | [PMC fining agents](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3354224/) |
| Eggs of other birds (duck, goose, quail) cross-react | documented | [FARE egg](https://www.foodallergy.org/living-food-allergy/food-allergy-essentials/common-allergens/egg) |

### 5.3 Guard phrases
"egg-free", "vegan", "plant-based", "eggless mayo / vegan mayo". Note
that "lecithin" is usually soy but can be egg (Mayo lists lecithin under
egg terms): a bare "lecithin" is *possible* for both.

### 5.4 Cross-contact
Flattop grill (eggs at breakfast; [FDA CPG 555.250](https://www.fda.gov/media/168000/download));
shared fryers with battered items; "it is impossible to separate the egg
white completely from the yolk" (FARE) — "yolk only" is no guard.

### 5.5 Confusions
- Baked (well-cooked) egg is tolerated by many but raw and lightly cooked
  egg is not (Allergy UK): a menu matcher cannot tell, so all forms stay
  triggers with a severity note.
- Egg synonyms are not required to say "egg" outside the regulated list
  rules ([FARE egg](https://www.foodallergy.org/living-food-allergy/food-allergy-essentials/common-allergens/egg)).

## 6. Soy

**Regulated:** US 9 (soybeans), EU 14 (with four exemptions incl. fully
refined soybean oil), Israel (סויה).

### 6.1 Direct triggers
soy, soya, soybean, edamame, miso, natto, shoyu, soy sauce, tamari,
tempeh, tofu, bean curd, yuba, textured vegetable protein (TVP), soy
protein (isolate / concentrate), soy flour, soy milk, soy lecithin (weak),
soybean oil (weak)
([FARE soy](https://www.foodallergy.org/living-food-allergy/food-allergy-essentials/common-allergens/soy),
[Anaphylaxis UK soya](https://anaphylaxis.org.uk/factsheets/soya-allergy/)).

### 6.2 Hidden sources

| Hidden source | Confidence | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **Vegetable broth, vegetable gum, vegetable starch** | documented common | [FARE soy](https://www.foodallergy.org/living-food-allergy/food-allergy-essentials/common-allergens/soy) |
| **Vegetable oil, vegetable paste, hydrolysed plant/vegetable protein (HVP)** | documented common | [Allergy UK soya](https://www.allergyuk.org/resources/soya-allergy-factsheet/), [Anaphylaxis UK soya](https://anaphylaxis.org.uk/factsheets/soya-allergy/) |
| Asian cuisine — "even a soy-free menu item carries a high risk of cross-contact" | documented common | [FARE soy](https://www.foodallergy.org/living-food-allergy/food-allergy-essentials/common-allergens/soy) |
| Baked goods, canned broths and soups, canned tuna and meat, processed meats, sauces, sausages, protein bars | documented common | [FARE soy](https://www.foodallergy.org/living-food-allergy/food-allergy-essentials/common-allergens/soy) |
| "Hydrolyzed vegetable protein, textured vegetable protein, hydrolyzed plant protein, monodiglyceride" | documented | [Mayo soy](http://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/soy-allergy/basics/symptoms/con-20031370) |
| Lecithin contaminated with soy protein (case report) | possible | [PMC case](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4469404/) |

**Oil and lecithin:** highly refined soybean oil is exempt from US
labelling and tolerated by most ([FDA CPG 555.250](https://www.fda.gov/media/168000/download),
[Mayo soy](http://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/soy-allergy/basics/symptoms/con-20031370));
the EU exempts "fully refined soybean oil and fat"; most soy-allergic
people tolerate lecithin ([Allergy UK soya](https://www.allergyuk.org/resources/soya-allergy-factsheet/)).
Cold-pressed/expelled soy oil should be avoided (FARE). Model "soybean
oil" and "soy lecithin" as *possible*, user-configurable.

### 6.3 Guard phrases
"soy-free", "no soy", "coconut aminos" (a soy-sauce substitute — inferred,
not sourced; mark possible), "tamari" is **not** a soy guard (it is soy;
it is a *wheat* guard, §7).

### 6.4 Cross-contact
Asian kitchens: woks and oil shared with soy-sauce dishes
([Coeliac UK takeaways](https://www.coeliac.org.uk/living-with-coeliac-disease/food-and-drink/eating-out-and-travel/16528-2/)
— written for gluten in soy sauce; same pan, same risk).

### 6.5 Confusions
- Soy sauce is a **wheat/gluten** trigger as much as a soy one: Allergy UK
  notes most of the protein in soy sauce comes from fermented wheat;
  FARE and Mayo list soy sauce under wheat.
- "Vegetable ___" words hide soy (and pea, §16): "vegetable protein",
  "vegetable broth", "vegetable oil".
- Peanut-allergic people may react to soy (legume cross-reactivity, §16).

## 7. Wheat and gluten-containing cereals

**Regulated:** US 9 lists **wheat only** (barley and rye are not US
allergens); EU 14 "cereals containing gluten, namely wheat (such as spelt
and khorasan wheat), rye, barley, oats or their hybridised strains";
Israel (דגנים המכילים גלוטן; חיטה, שעורה, שיפון, שיבולת שועל).

### 7.1 Direct triggers
**Wheat forms (FARE):** wheat, bulgur, couscous, durum, einkorn, emmer,
farina, farro, freekeh, kamut / khorasan, seitan, semolina, spelt,
triticale, wheat starch, wheat bran/germ
([FARE wheat](https://www.foodallergy.org/living-food-allergies/food-allergy-essentials/common-allergens/wheat)).
**Other gluten cereals (Coeliac UK, CDF):** barley, pearl barley, rye,
malt, malted barley, malt extract/syrup/flavouring/vinegar, brewer's
yeast, beer / lager / ale / stout, barley squash
([Coeliac UK grains](https://www.coeliac.org.uk/grains/),
[CDF sources](https://celiac.org/gluten-free-living/what-is-gluten/sources-of-gluten/)).
**Dish-level:** bread, pita, bun, pastry, batter, breadcrumbs, croutons,
pasta, noodles, pizza, flour tortilla, dumplings, pancake — and the
Israeli bakery words in file 12.

### 7.2 Hidden sources

| Hidden source | Confidence | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **Soy sauce**; glucose syrup; starch (gelatinised, modified, food, vegetable starch); surimi; plant-based meats | documented common | [FARE wheat](https://www.foodallergy.org/living-food-allergies/food-allergy-essentials/common-allergens/wheat) |
| Hot dogs, imitation crab, processed meats, sausages, salad dressings | documented common | [FARE wheat PDF](https://www.foodallergy.org/media/1706/download) |
| Hydrolysed vegetable protein, meat products, ice cream, natural flavourings, gelatinised / modified starch, vegetable gum — "wheat proteins, especially gluten, are used as food thickeners" | documented common | [Mayo wheat](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/wheat-allergy/symptoms-causes/syc-20378897) |
| Stock cubes, breadcrumbs, wheat flour in sauces, croutons in salad | documented common | [Coeliac UK about gluten](https://www.coeliac.org.uk/information-and-support/living-gluten-free/the-gluten-free-diet/about-gluten/) |
| Beer, lager, stout, ale, malted drinks | documented common | [Coeliac UK grains](https://www.coeliac.org.uk/grains/) |
| Malt vinegar, malt flavouring, brewer's yeast | documented common | [CDF sources](https://celiac.org/gluten-free-living/what-is-gluten/sources-of-gluten/) |
| Omelettes with pancake batter; baked potatoes coated in flour; sauces with flour, soy or teriyaki | documented common | [CDF dining](https://celiac.org/gluten-free-living/dining-and-social-eating/) |
| Oats not labelled gluten-free (cross-contact in harvest/milling); some coeliacs also react to avenin | documented common | [Coeliac UK oats](https://www.coeliac.org.uk/app/uploads/oats-factsheet-nov-2024.pdf), [CDF](https://celiac.org/gluten-free-living/what-is-gluten/sources-of-gluten/) |
| Einkorn sold as if not wheat (2022 recall) | documented | [FARE wheat](https://www.foodallergy.org/living-food-allergies/food-allergy-essentials/common-allergens/wheat) |
| Lupin flour used in gluten-free products (a *lupin* hide for gluten-free eaters, §14) | documented | [FDA lupin FAQ](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/lupin-and-allergenicity-frequently-asked-questions) |

**Exempt derivatives (EU Annex II; Coeliac UK):** wheat-based glucose
syrups incl. dextrose, wheat-based maltodextrins, barley-based glucose
syrups, cereals in distilled spirits — safe for coeliacs even when
"wheat" is in the name ([Coeliac UK FAQs](https://www.coeliac.org.uk/about-coeliac-disease/living-gluten-free-faqs/)).
CDF is inconsistent on distilled vinegar (one brochure lists it as safe
and as a source) — mark *possible*. Small amounts of barley malt vinegar
≤20 ppm are allowed by Coeliac UK.

### 7.3 Guard phrases
"gluten-free" / "GF" / "ללא גלוטן" (Israel has had Gluten Marking
regulations since 1996 — [gov.il food labelling](https://www.gov.il/en/departments/guides/food-labeling)),
"gluten-free bun / pasta / pizza base", "tamari (wheat-free)", "rice
noodles", "corn tortilla", "buckwheat" (not wheat), "quinoa", "rice
flour". **Weak guards:** "wheat-free" (7.5), "oats" without "gluten-free",
"GF pizza" in a wheat-flour kitchen (CDF: 32 % of restaurant foods
labelled gluten-free had detectable gluten; pizza and pasta the worst —
[CDF study](https://celiac.org/2018/10/10/hidden-gluten-in-restaurant-foods-labeled-gluten-free/)).

### 7.4 Cross-contact (the best-documented of all)
- **Fryer:** separate fryer or clean oil; shallow-frying oil never used
  for gluten foods ([Coeliac UK catering guidance](https://www.coeliac.org.uk/app/uploads/1catering-guidancedoc-dec-2019-2.pdf));
  "fried foods cooked in oil shared with breaded products" ([CDF](https://celiac.org/gluten-free-living/what-is-gluten/sources-of-gluten/));
  chip shops with a dedicated GF fryer or GF nights ([Coeliac UK eating out](https://www.coeliac.org.uk/living-with-coeliac-disease/food-and-drink/eating-out-and-travel/hints-and-tips-for-eating-out/)).
- **Toaster:** dedicated toaster or toaster bags (Coeliac UK; CDF).
- **Flour dust:** "a floury environment"; separate by time, distance and
  equipment ([Coeliac UK catering poster](https://www.coeliac.org.uk/app/uploads/gf-catering-poster-top-10-23-06-2022.pdf)).
- **Pasta water:** use fresh water; water that cooked gluten pasta is a
  listed risk (Coeliac UK catering guidance).
- **Wok / pan:** Chinese dishes "cooked in the same pan" or in oil used
  for gluten foods ([Coeliac UK takeaways](https://www.coeliac.org.uk/living-with-coeliac-disease/food-and-drink/eating-out-and-travel/16528-2/)).
- **Boards, colanders, sifters, double-dipped condiments** (CDF; Coeliac UK).
- **Grill:** French toast on the breakfast flattop ([FDA CPG 555.250](https://www.fda.gov/media/168000/download)).

### 7.5 Confusions
- **"Wheat-free" is not "gluten-free"** — may still contain barley, rye
  or spelt ([Beyond Celiac](https://www.beyondceliac.org/gluten-free-diet/common-food-safety-questions/food-safety-wheat-free-vs-gluten-free/),
  [CDF label reading](https://celiac.org/gluten-free-living/gluten-free-foods/label-reading-the-fda/)).
- **Buckwheat is not wheat** — naturally gluten-free (CDF; FARE).
- **Wheat allergy ≠ coeliac disease ≠ gluten sensitivity**: a wheat-allergic
  person can eat barley and rye; a coeliac cannot. Two vocabularies: a
  *wheat* set and a *gluten* superset.
- **Spelt and kamut are wheat** (EU wording; FARE); "ancient grain" is not
  a guard.
- **Oats**: only "gluten-free oats" are a guard; plain oats are a
  cross-contact trigger for coeliacs and avenin reactors (Coeliac UK).
- **Tamari** is a wheat guard but a soy trigger (§6).
- Thresholds: "gluten-free" means ≤ 20 ppm in the US (FDA) and the UK/EU
  (Coeliac UK).

fish, any species name (salmon, tuna, cod, anchovy, sardine, mackerel,

### 8.2 Hidden sources

### 8.3 Guard phrases
"fish-free", "vegan Caesar", "vegetarian Worcestershire", "vegan fish
sauce". A "vegetarian" dish is only a weak guard where Worcestershire or
Caesar is involved (anchovy is routinely overlooked as "not meat").

### 8.4 Cross-contact
"Even if you order a non-fish item off the menu, cross-contact of fish
protein is possible"; fish protein "can become airborne in the steam
released during cooking" ([FARE fish](https://www.foodallergy.org/living-food-allergies/food-allergy-essentials/common-allergens/fish));
same batter or oil, e.g. fish and chips ([Allergy UK](https://www.allergyuk.org/resources/fish-and-shellfish-allergy-factsheet/));
FARE suggests avoiding seafood restaurants altogether
([FARE, Dining Out](https://www.foodallergy.org/resources/dining-out)).

### 8.5 Confusions
- Fish allergy does **not** raise the risk of shellfish allergy; having
  both is usually coincidence ([Anaphylaxis UK fish](https://anaphylaxis.org.uk/factsheets/fish-allergy/)).
  Model fish, crustaceans and molluscs as three restrictions.
- US "fish" means finfish only; molluscs are unregulated there
  ([FDA Chapter 19](https://www.fda.gov/media/80337/download)).
- Isinglass in beer/wine is exempt from EU labelling but is still fish —
  a strict user may want it as *possible*.

### 9.2 Hidden sources

| Hidden source | Confidence | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Fish stock; seafood flavouring (crab or clam extract); fish sauce "sometimes made from krill"; surimi; bouillabaisse; cuttlefish ink | documented common | [FARE shellfish](https://www.foodallergy.org/living-food-allergy/food-allergy-essentials/common-allergens/shellfish) |

### 9.3 Guard phrases

### 9.4 Cross-contact

shared pans, oil and utensils; "inhaling steam from cooking shellfish"
([Mayo shellfish](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/shellfish-allergy/symptoms-causes/syc-20377503));
Anaphylaxis UK: reactions to shellfish cooking vapour are usually mild
([Anaphylaxis UK shellfish](https://www.anaphylaxis.org.uk/fact-sheet/shellfish-allergy/)).

### 9.5 Confusions
- Shrimp is the most common trigger; crustacean allergy is more common
  than mollusc allergy, and clinical cross-reactivity between the two is
  "likely to be relatively low" (FSANZ) but a 2023 review advises
  avoiding all shellfish ([FSANZ safety assessment](https://www.foodstandards.gov.au/sites/default/files/food-standards-code/proposals/Documents/P1044%20SD2%20-%20Safety%20Assessment.pdf),
  [PMC review](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10386692/)).
  Default the UI to "shellfish" = crustaceans + molluscs with an opt-out.
- Shellfish allergy is not iodine allergy (FARE).

| Hidden source | Confidence | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **Mayonnaise, salad dressings, "chicken dip", sauces** — most Canadian case reports involved mustard hidden in other sauces | documented common | [Health Canada systematic review](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/food-nutrition/reports-publications/food-labelling/mustard-priority-food-allergen-canada-systematic-review.html) |
| Curries, chutneys, pickles and pickled products; curry powder; pickled onions (seeds in the vinegar), gherkins, piccalilli | documented common | [Health Canada mustard](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/food-nutrition/reports-publications/food-safety/mustard-priority-food-allergen.html), [Allergy UK mustard](https://www.allergyuk.org/resources/mustard-allergy-factsheet/) |
| Meat coatings and marinades; a ham glaze (Canadian case) | documented common | [Allergy UK mustard](https://www.allergyuk.org/resources/mustard-allergy-factsheet/), [Health Canada](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/food-nutrition/reports-publications/food-safety/mustard-priority-food-allergen.html) |

### 13.3 Guard phrases
"mustard-free", "no mustard". No cooking guard: mustard proteins "are

### 13.4 Cross-contact
Not specifically documented; dressings and marinades are the vector.

### 13.5 Confusions
- Unregulated in the US: "house dressing" there is a mandatory ask.
- Mustard greens/leaves count (Allergy UK).

## 14. Lupin

**Regulated:** EU 14, FSANZ (since 2018), Israel (תורמוס). **Not** US
(FDA: FALCPA's list "doesn't include lupin") or Canada.

### 14.1 Direct triggers
lupin, lupine, lupini beans, tremoços / altramuces (Mediterranean bar
snack), lupin flour, lupin protein, lupin bran, lupin seeds; Hebrew תורמוס
([Anaphylaxis UK lupin](https://anaphylaxis.org.uk/factsheets/lupin-allergy/),
[FDA lupin FAQ](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/lupin-and-allergenicity-frequently-asked-questions)).

### 14.2 Hidden sources

| Hidden source | Confidence | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **Lupin flour in baked goods and pasta, "especially gluten-free products"** | documented common | [FDA lupin FAQ](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/lupin-and-allergenicity-frequently-asked-questions) |

### 14.4 Cross-contact

- Lupin is a **gluten-free** bakery ingredient: the gluten-free guard
  raises lupin risk.

## 15. Sulphites (sulphur dioxide)

**Regulated:** EU 14 and UK, Canada, FSANZ, Israel — all at **≥ 10 mg/kg or
10 mg/L** of total SO₂ (EU: "more than 10"; FSANZ/Canada: "10 or more").
Not a US "major food allergen" (FDA regulates sulphite declaration
separately as an additive — not covered by the snippets reviewed).
Hebrew: דו-תחמוצת הגופרית וסולפיטים / גופרית דו חמצנית (2022 draft wording).

### 15.1 Direct triggers
sulphite(s) / sulfite(s), sulphur dioxide, **E220–E228** (E220 sulphur
dioxide, E221 sodium sulphite, E222 sodium bisulphite, E223 sodium
metabisulphite, E224 potassium metabisulphite, E226 calcium sulphite,
E227 calcium hydrogen sulphite, E228 potassium hydrogen sulphite — the
range per Anaphylaxis UK; the individual names are standard E-number
names, verify), "contains sulphites" (wine)
([Anaphylaxis UK sulphites](https://anaphylaxis.org.uk/factsheets/sulphites/)).

### 15.2 Hidden sources

| Hidden source | Confidence | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **Wine** ("most wines"), beer, cider | documented common | [Allergy UK sulphites](https://www.allergyuk.org/resources/sulphites-and-airway-symptoms-factsheet/), [Anaphylaxis UK](https://anaphylaxis.org.uk/factsheets/sulphites/) |
| **Dried fruit** (apricots, prunes, raisins) and dried vegetables | documented common | [Allergy UK](https://www.allergyuk.org/resources/sulphites-and-airway-symptoms-factsheet/), [Anaphylaxis UK](https://anaphylaxis.org.uk/factsheets/sulphites/) |
| **Dehydrated, pre-cut or processed potatoes** (chips/fries from pre-prepared potato) | documented common | [Allergy UK](https://www.allergyuk.org/resources/sulphites-and-airway-symptoms-factsheet/) |
| **Prawns** and some processed seafood | documented common | [Allergy UK](https://www.allergyuk.org/resources/sulphites-and-airway-symptoms-factsheet/) |
| Pickled foods and **vinegar** | documented common | [Allergy UK](https://www.allergyuk.org/resources/sulphites-and-airway-symptoms-factsheet/) |
| Processed meats incl. sausages and burgers; salad dressings; ready-made mustard; soft drinks | documented common | [Anaphylaxis UK sulphites PDF](https://www.anaphylaxis.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2022/10/Sulphites-V8-Oct-25-neffy-update.pdf) |
| Fruit juices, cordials, guacamole, fresh salad on salad bars (anti-browning) | possible — standard sulphite uses, not in the snippets reviewed | — |

### 15.3 Guards
"sulphite-free", "no added sulphites", "organic wine" (**not** a guard —
may still contain sulphites; inferred). The UK rule covers deliberately
added sulphites only, but analysis cannot distinguish natural ones
([FSA technical guidance part 1](https://www.food.gov.uk/business-guidance/food-allergen-labelling-and-information-requirements-technical-guidance-part-1-guidance-for-businesses-providing-prepacked-food)).

### 15.4 Cross-contact
Not relevant: sulphites are a dose-threshold intolerance, not a trace
allergen.

### 15.5 Confusions
- True IgE sulphite allergy is "very rare"; sensitivity is < 2 % of the
  population but 5–13 % of asthmatics; most reactions are non-allergic
  ([Allergy UK](https://www.allergyuk.org/resources/sulphites-and-airway-symptoms-factsheet/),
  [Anaphylaxis UK](https://anaphylaxis.org.uk/factsheets/sulphites/)).
  Present sulphites as an intolerance with a threshold, like lactose.
- "Sulfa" drug allergy is unrelated (no source reviewed — product note;
  verify before stating in-app).

## 16. Legumes (chickpea, lentil, pea, fava and others)

**Regulated:** nowhere as a group. Peanut, soy and lupin are regulated;
"all other legumes and pulses (including lentils, peas and chickpeas) are
NOT included in the UK Top 14" ([Allergy UK legumes](https://www.allergyuk.org/resources/reactions-to-legumes/)).
Israel adds a **fava (פול) G6PD statement** that is explicitly *not* an
allergen marking (§17.3).

### 16.1 Direct triggers
chickpea / garbanzo, hummus (חומוס), falafel (chickpea or fava), lentil
(dal / dhal), pea, split pea, green pea, pea protein, fava / broad bean
(פול — ful medames), bean (kidney, black, cannellini, haricot, mung,
adzuki), cowpea / black-eyed pea, fenugreek, carob (legume — inferred,
possible), tamarind (legume — inferred, possible), soy (§6), lupin
(§14), peanut (§1)
([Allergy UK legumes](https://www.allergyuk.org/resources/reactions-to-legumes/),
[Anaphylaxis UK legumes](https://www.anaphylaxis.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2022/10/Legume-Factsheet-V10-Final-2025.pdf)).

### 16.2 Hidden sources

| Hidden source | Confidence | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **Pea protein in plant-based meat substitutes, protein powders/shakes, dairy alternatives**; may hide inside "vegetable protein" / "vegetable fibre" | documented common | [Allergy UK pea protein](https://www.allergyuk.org/resources/reactions-to-pea-protein/) |
| Pea-protein veggie burger reaction in a peanut-allergic child (label: "no peanuts or tree nuts") | documented | [PMC pea case series](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10509857/) |
| Lentils, chickpeas and peas as "emerging allergens" from growing use in everyday foods | documented | [Anaphylaxis UK legumes](https://www.anaphylaxis.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2022/10/Legume-Factsheet-V10-Final-2025.pdf) |
| Chickpea/lentil allergy commoner in India, the Middle East and Mediterranean countries where they are staples | documented | [Allergy UK legumes](https://www.allergyuk.org/resources/reactions-to-legumes/) |
| Gram / besan flour (chickpea) in batters (pakora, socca), falafel mixes with fava, hummus as a side or spread | possible — standard uses, not in the snippets reviewed; Israeli instances in file 12 | — |

### 16.3 Guards
None reliable: nothing obliges a menu to name a legume. "Vegetable
protein" is a *trigger* (Allergy UK), not neutral. Anaphylaxis UK says a
deliberately added single legume must not be hidden under "vegetable" —
the two charities conflict, so treat "vegetable protein" as *possible*.

### 16.4 Cross-contact
Not documented for legumes specifically.

### 16.5 Confusions
- Cross-reactivity "varies by person" and "a reaction to one legume
  doesn't mean all legumes must be avoided" (Allergy UK; Anaphylaxis UK) —
  model each legume as its own toggle under a "legumes" parent.
- Peanut-allergic → lupin, soy, pea, chickpea, fenugreek, beans, lentils in
  "a small number" (Anaphylaxis UK); most cross-reactions are IgE to
  homologous proteins, e.g. peanut–lupin ([PMC legume review](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11364600/)).
- **Fava / G6PD is not an allergy** (favism is haemolysis in G6PD
  deficiency — Maariv, §17.3) and should be a separate restriction.
- Hummus = sesame **and** chickpea; falafel = chickpea and/or fava, often
  served with tahini.

**Regulated:** nowhere. CDC: cow's milk is the only alpha-gal-containing
ingredient that is a major food allergen.

### 17.1 Direct triggers
beef, pork, lamb, mutton, venison, goat, rabbit, bison; organ meats
(liver, lung, heart, kidney, tripe, sweetbreads, scrapple, "Rocky
Mountain oysters"); lard, tallow, suet, dripping; gelatin (beef/pork);
meat broth, bouillon, stock, gravy; sausage casings (even on poultry
sausages); glycerin, magnesium stearate, bovine extract
([CDC products list](https://www.cdc.gov/alpha-gal-syndrome/data-research/products-containing-alpha-gal/index.html),
[CDC managing](https://www.cdc.gov/alpha-gal-syndrome/managing/index.html),
[Mayo Clinic Press](https://mcpress.mayoclinic.org/living-well/living-with-alpha-gal-syndrome/)).

### 17.2 Hidden sources (restaurant)

| Hidden source | Confidence | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Foods cooked in mammal fat (lard, tallow, suet) — e.g. fries, pastry, refried beans; meat broth/stock/gravy | documented common | [CDC products](https://www.cdc.gov/alpha-gal-syndrome/data-research/products-containing-alpha-gal/index.html) |
| Gelatin in desserts (panna cotta, marshmallow, jelly, gummies) | documented | [CDC products](https://www.cdc.gov/alpha-gal-syndrome/data-research/products-containing-alpha-gal/index.html) |
| Sausage casings on non-mammal sausages | documented | [Mayo Clinic Press](https://mcpress.mayoclinic.org/living-well/living-with-alpha-gal-syndrome/) |
| High-fat dairy (ice cream, whole cream) — many tolerate dairy, some do not | documented, individual | [CDC managing](https://www.cdc.gov/alpha-gal-syndrome/managing/index.html), [AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/allergist-resources/ask-the-expert/answers/old-ask-the-experts/alpha-gal) |
| Carrageenan | not confirmed — no source reviewed mentions it; mark possible | — |

### 17.3 Guards
Poultry (chicken, turkey, duck, quail), fish, seafood, eggs, fruit and
vegetables are "generally safe" (CDC). "Vegetarian" / "vegan" are strong
guards except for gelatin and lard in pastry/fries.

### 17.4 Cross-contact
Shared fryers and grills with mammal fat — inferred from §0; the CDC
page addresses products, not kitchens.

### 17.5 Confusions
- Reactions are delayed (hours) and dose/fat dependent; "people with AGS
  might not react to every product containing alpha-gal" (CDC).
- Pork gelatin and lard matter to kosher/halal users for a different
  reason; one lexicon entry, two restrictions (file 12).

## 19. The official lists

### 19.1 United States — the "major food allergens" (9)

FALCPA (2004) defined eight; the **FASTER Act**, signed 23 April 2021,
added sesame effective **1 January 2023**: **milk, eggs, fish, Crustacean
shellfish, tree nuts, peanuts, wheat, soybeans, sesame**
([FDA FASTER Act page](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-allergies/faster-act-sesame-ninth-major-food-allergen),
[FDA Food Allergies](https://www.fda.gov/food/nutrition-food-labeling-and-critical-foods/food-allergies),
[FDA FALCPA](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-allergensgluten-free-guidance-documents-regulatory-information/food-allergen-labeling-and-consumer-protection-act-2004-falcpa)).
Notes:
- "Fish" = finfish; molluscan shellfish are **not** covered
  ([FDA Chapter 19](https://www.fda.gov/media/80337/download)).
- **Highly refined oils** from a major allergen, and ingredients made
  from them, are excluded by statute (FD&C Act §201(qq)(2)(A))
  ([FDA CPG 555.250](https://www.fda.gov/media/168000/download)).
- The FDA **Tree Nut List** was revised in the 5th-edition FAQ (6 Jan
  2025): coconut and eleven others removed
  ([FDA FAQ](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-allergensgluten-free-guidance-documents-regulatory-information/frequently-asked-questions-food-allergen-labeling-guidance-industry)).
- Products in stock before 2023 need not declare sesame
  ([FDA FASTER Act page](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-allergies/faster-act-sesame-ninth-major-food-allergen)).
- Advisory ("may contain") statements are voluntary (FDA FAQ).
- The 2022 Food Code requires the person in charge to know the major
  allergens and cross-contact; adoption varies by state
  ([CDC MMWR](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/66/wr/mm6615a2.htm)).

### 19.2 European Union — Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011, Annex II (14)

**Caveat:** the authoritative text could not be fetched (EUR-Lex and
legislation.gov.uk both refused). The wording below is the consolidated
Annex II as republished and as confirmed piecemeal by the search snippets
of [legislation.gov.uk Annex II](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2011/1169/annex/II)
([PDF](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2011/1169/annex/II/data.pdf)),
[EUR-Lex consolidated 01.04.2025](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX%3A02011R1169-20250401)
and [University of Reading](https://www.reading.ac.uk/foodlaw/label/allergens-list.htm).
**Verify against EUR-Lex before quoting in-app.**

1. **Cereals containing gluten**, namely: wheat (such as spelt and
   khorasan wheat), rye, barley, oats or their hybridised strains, and
   products thereof, except: (a) wheat based glucose syrups including
   dextrose; (b) wheat based maltodextrins; (c) glucose syrups based on
   barley; (d) cereals used for making alcoholic distillates including
   ethyl alcohol of agricultural origin. *(Pre-2014 wording listed
   "spelt, kamut" by name — Reg. 78/2014 changed it.)*
2. **Crustaceans** and products thereof.
3. **Eggs** and products thereof.
4. **Fish** and products thereof, except: (a) fish gelatine used as
   carrier for vitamin or carotenoid preparations; (b) fish gelatine or
   Isinglass used as fining agent in beer and wine.
5. **Peanuts** and products thereof.
6. **Soybeans** and products thereof, except: (a) fully refined soybean
   oil and fat; (b) natural mixed tocopherols (E306), natural D-alpha
   tocopherol, natural D-alpha tocopherol acetate, and natural D-alpha
   tocopherol succinate from soybean sources; (c) vegetable oils derived
   phytosterols and phytosterol esters from soybean sources; (d) plant
   stanol ester produced from vegetable oil sterols from soybean sources.
7. **Milk** and products thereof (including lactose), except: (a) whey
   used for making alcoholic distillates including ethyl alcohol of
   agricultural origin; (b) lactitol.
8. **Nuts**, namely: almonds (*Amygdalus communis* L.), hazelnuts
   (*Corylus avellana*), walnuts (*Juglans regia*), cashews (*Anacardium
   occidentale*), pecan nuts (*Carya illinoinensis* (Wangenh.) K. Koch),
   Brazil nuts (*Bertholletia excelsa*), pistachio nuts (*Pistacia vera*),
   macadamia or Queensland nuts (*Macadamia ternifolia*), and products
   thereof, except for nuts used for making alcoholic distillates
   including ethyl alcohol of agricultural origin.
9. **Celery** and products thereof.
10. **Mustard** and products thereof.
11. **Sesame seeds** and products thereof.
12. **Sulphur dioxide and sulphites** at concentrations of more than
    10 mg/kg or 10 mg/litre in terms of the total SO₂ which are to be
    calculated for products as proposed ready for consumption or as
    reconstituted according to the instructions of the manufacturers.
13. **Lupin** and products thereof.
14. **Molluscs** and products thereof.

Applies to non-prepacked food (restaurants) in the EU/UK (Article 44;
re-checked 2026-10-19,
[legislation.gov.uk Article 44](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2011/1169/article/44):
the allergen particulars are mandatory for non-prepacked food, the means
are national); the FSA says
loose food must have allergen information available for every item,
best practice in writing (March 2025 guidance, not law; oral information
with a sign still meets the legal minimum) ([FSA allergen guidance](https://www.food.gov.uk/business-guidance/allergen-guidance-for-food-businesses),
[FSA non-prepacked](https://www.food.gov.uk/business-guidance/allergen-information-for-non-prepacked-foods-best-practice-approach)).

**The fava / G6PD marking.** Press reports of the reform: fava is *not*

## 21. FODMAP (ALG-17; desk research, brief)

> **Status: desk research from the author's background reading of the
> public sources named below, added on 2026-10-15 for ALG-17. Not clinical
> review. Checked 2026-10-19 against web search results (no page could be
> opened in full): Gibson & Shepherd 2010 is real as cited (PMID 20136989,
> [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20136989/)) and says the diet
> needs a trained dietitian; NICE's advice is that a low-FODMAP diet be
> given only by "a healthcare professional with expertise in dietary
> management" ([NICE QS114 statement 3](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/QS114/chapter/quality-statement-3-dietary-management),
> the quality standard built on CG61; whether CG61 itself has been
> replaced was not confirmed); Monash's own page
> ([monashfodmap.com](https://www.monashfodmap.com/about-fodmap-and-ibs/high-and-low-fodmap-foods))
> and dietitian summaries of it confirm onion, garlic, leek, shallot,
> apple, pear, mango, watermelon, honey, cauliflower, mushrooms, cashews
> and pistachios, and that chickpeas are dose dependent. The §21.2 rows
> marked "inferred" remain unreviewed.**

**Regulated:** nowhere. FODMAPs (fermentable oligo-, di-, monosaccharides
and polyols) are not allergens; a low-FODMAP diet is a short, staged
elimination for irritable bowel syndrome that guidance says to follow with
a dietitian (NICE CG61; NHS IBS page; Monash University). **Dose is the
whole story**: Monash rates each food per serving size, and a small amount
of a "high" food may be tolerated (Gibson & Shepherd 2010). A menu never
states a dose, so the app may only say "may be high in …" and never rules a
dish out (file 10 §ALG-17).

### 21.1 Direct triggers (high-FODMAP foods a menu names)
onion, shallot, leek, garlic (fructans); chickpeas, lentils, kidney and
baked beans (GOS); apple, pear, mango, watermelon, honey, agave, high
fructose corn syrup (excess fructose); milk, yoghurt, ice cream, ricotta,
cottage cheese (lactose); cauliflower, mushrooms, apricot, peach, plum,
cherries, nectarine, sorbitol, mannitol, xylitol (polyols); asparagus,
artichoke, cashews, pistachios, inulin / chicory root
([Monash, high and low FODMAP foods](https://www.monashfodmap.com/about-fodmap-and-ibs/high-and-low-fodmap-foods/),
Gibson PR, Shepherd SJ, *J Gastroenterol Hepatol* 2010;25:252–258).

### 21.2 Hidden sources (restaurant)

| Hidden source | Confidence | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Stock, broth, soup and gravy (onion and garlic are the usual base) | possible (inferred from 21.1) | Monash, above |
| Garlic-based sauces and dips: aioli, tzatziki, pesto, garlic butter | documented common (garlic named) | Monash, above |
| Hummus and falafel (chickpea, garlic) | documented common | Monash, above |
| Salsa, marinades, dressings (onion, garlic, honey) | possible | inferred |
| Wheat breads, pita, pasta and couscous in a full serving (fructans) | possible — dose dependent | Monash, above |
| Date syrup (silan) and fruit juices (excess fructose) | possible | Monash, above |

### 21.3 Guards
"Green beans" are not the beans of 21.1, and plant milks (almond, oat,
rice) are not lactose sources; "lactose-free" lowers the milk trigger. A
"low FODMAP" label on a menu is a restaurant's own claim.

### 21.4 Confusions
- FODMAP is not gluten: a gluten-free bread may still be high in fructans
  from other flours, and a sourdough wheat bread may be lower (Monash).
- The diet is temporary and individual; the app's heads-up is "based on
  common FODMAP guidance, not a measurement".

[Monash University FODMAP](https://www.monashfodmap.com/about-fodmap-and-ibs/high-and-low-fodmap-foods/);

## 22. Histamine (ALG-17; desk research, brief)

> **Status:** as §21 — background reading of the named sources, not
> clinical review. **Checked 2026-10-19:** Maintz & Novak 2007 is real as
> cited (*Am J Clin Nutr* 85(5):1185–1196, PMID 17490952) and defines
> histamine intolerance as a disequilibrium of histamine and the capacity
> to degrade it, with diamine oxidase the main enzyme
> ([nutrition-evidence.com](https://nutrition-evidence.com/article/99495/histamine-and-histamine-intolerance));
> SIGHI's list exists, distinguishes histamine-rich foods from
> "liberators" and sorts foods into three tolerance groups
> ([SIGHI downloads](https://histaminintoleranz.ch/en/downloads.html)).
> **Unconfirmed:** SIGHI's rating of each food in §22.1–§22.2 (tomato,
> spinach, strawberries, aged cheeses): the list itself could not be
> opened, so those rows rest on the 2026-10-15 reading.

**Regulated:** nowhere. Histamine intolerance is a proposed imbalance
between dietary histamine and the body's capacity to break it down (the
enzyme diamine oxidase); its diagnosis is debated, the threshold is
individual, and histamine rises with ripening, fermentation, curing and
storage, so freshness and preparation matter as much as the food (Maintz
& Novak 2007; Comas-Basté et al. 2020). The app may only say "may be high
in …".

### 22.1 Direct triggers
aged and ripened cheeses (parmesan, cheddar, gouda, emmental, blue
cheese, camembert, brie); cured, smoked and processed meats (salami,
pepperoni, ham, prosciutto, bacon, chorizo, pastrami); tuna, mackerel,
sardines, anchovies, herring and smoked fish; fermented foods (sauerkraut,
kimchi, pickles, miso, tempeh, kombucha, kefir); vinegar (balsamic, wine
vinegar); wine, beer, champagne
([SIGHI food list](https://www.histaminintoleranz.ch/en/therapy_diet_foodlist.html);
Maintz L, Novak N, *Am J Clin Nutr* 2007;85:1185–1196).

### 22.2 Hidden sources and "liberators"

| Hidden source | Confidence | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Soy sauce, fish sauce, Worcestershire sauce, ketchup (fermented or vinegar based) | documented common | SIGHI list; Maintz & Novak 2007 |
| Pesto and Caesar dressing (aged parmesan; anchovy) | possible (inferred from 22.1) | — |
| Tomato (and tomato paste), spinach, aubergine, avocado | documented (histamine-rich vegetables) — dose dependent | SIGHI list; Comas-Basté et al. 2020 |
| Strawberries, pineapple, chocolate and cocoa ("liberators") | possible — the mechanism is debated | SIGHI list |
| Leftovers and long-held fish | documented, but invisible on a menu | Maintz & Novak 2007 |

### 22.3 Guards
"Fresh" beside a fish does not lower anything the app can check; there is
no reliable guard phrase, so every trigger stays a heads-up.

### 22.4 Confusions
- Scombroid fish poisoning (spoiled fish) is histamine too, but it is a
  food-hygiene matter, not this restriction.
- Histamine intolerance is not an allergy: no cross-contact rule applies.

**Sources (22):**
[SIGHI, food compatibility list](https://www.histaminintoleranz.ch/en/therapy_diet_foodlist.html);
Maintz L, Novak N. "Histamine and histamine intolerance." *Am J Clin Nutr*
2007;85(5):1185–1196; Comas-Basté O, Sánchez-Pérez S, Veciana-Nogués MT,
Latorre-Moratalla M, Vidal-Carou MC. "Histamine Intolerance: The Current
State of the Art." *Biomolecules* 2020;10(8):1181.

## 24. Kosher components (issue #342; desk research, brief)

> **Status: desk research, not rabbinic review. Checked 2026-10-21** from
> search-result snippets only, as the banner above describes (no page was
> fetched in full: `chabad.org` and `thejc.com` refused the fetch from this
> environment). **Components only, never certification** (file 03,
> "Kosher, halal, Jain"; file 10's non-goals): a menu can name pork,
> shrimp or a steak in butter; it can never show that the meat was
> slaughtered and prepared under supervision or that the kitchen keeps
> meat and dairy apart. The app flags the components a menu names and asks
> about the certificate; it never says a dish or a kitchen is kosher. The
> only kosher statement it makes stays ALG-09's attributed chip, "Declared
> kosher by the platform".

**Regulated:** nowhere as an allergen. Kosher is a religious rule, and
whether a restaurant is kosher is a certificate (in Israel a rabbinate
*teudat kashrut*), which only the restaurant can show.

### 24.1 Pork
Pork and every pig product is forbidden: a land mammal is kosher only if it
both has split hooves and chews the cud, and the pig does not
([Yale Chaplain's Office](https://chaplain.yale.edu/sites/default/files/2024-10/Jewish%20Dietary%20Restrictions%20Reference%20Sheet.pdf) **[M]**;
[Wikipedia, Kosher foods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kosher_foods) **[M]**;
[University of Kentucky extension FCS3-577](https://publications.ca.uky.edu/sites/publications.ca.uky.edu/files/FCS3577.pdf) **[M]**).
Direct: pork, pig, piglet, suckling pig, boar, bacon, ham, gammon, lard,
lardo, lardons, carnitas, char siu, tonkatsu, tonkotsu, porchetta,
guanciale, prosciutto, pancetta, jamón, serrano ham. Likely: chorizo,
mortadella, speck, bratwurst, kielbasa, chicharrón, spare ribs, baby back
ribs. Suspected (often beef or turkey on an Israeli menu): salami,
pepperoni, sausage, hot dog. The lists are the app's culinary lexicon, not
sourced dish by dish; the rule they apply is.

### 24.2 Shellfish and fish without fins and scales
Of water creatures only those with both fins and removable scales are
kosher; shrimp, lobster, crab, oysters and every mollusc are not
([Yale](https://chaplain.yale.edu/sites/default/files/2024-10/Jewish%20Dietary%20Restrictions%20Reference%20Sheet.pdf) **[M]**;
[UKY FCS3-577](https://publications.ca.uky.edu/sites/publications.ca.uky.edu/files/FCS3577.pdf) **[M]**;
[MyJewishLearning, kosher fish](https://www.myjewishlearning.com/article/kosher-fish-list/) **[M]**).
The shellfish words are the Crustaceans and Molluscs vocabularies of §9
and §10, reused. Fish with no removable scales: catfish (and the
pangasius, basa and swai sold as catfish), eel, shark
([Chabad, non-kosher fish list, reprinting the OU's](https://www.chabad.org/library/article_cdo/aid/2998673/jewish/Non-Kosher-Fish-List.htm) **[M]**),
monkfish ([Chabad, "Is monkfish kosher"](https://www.chabad.org/library/article_cdo/aid/4660914/jewish/Is-Monkfish-Kosher.htm) **[M]**)
and skate ([Kashrut Authority of Australia](https://www.ka.org.au/consumer-resources/common-kosher-and-non-kosher-fish) **[M]**;
London Beth Din via [isitkosherapp](https://isitkosherapp.com/product/fish/fish-general-remark/1181163) **[W]**).
**Sturgeon** has ganoid scales that cannot be removed without tearing the
skin, so it is not kosher, and **caviar** is sturgeon roe
([Chabad, "Which scales make a fish kosher"](https://www.chabad.org/library/article_cdo/aid/6379285/jewish/Which-Scales-Make-a-Fish-Kosher.htm) **[M]**):
likely, since "salmon caviar" and "eggplant caviar" are something else.
**Swordfish** is disputed — "not accepted as kosher by contemporary
certifying agencies", while the Conservative movement accepts it and
sturgeon (same Chabad page; [MyJewishLearning](https://www.myjewishlearning.com/article/kosher-fish-list/) **[M]**):
suspected.

### 24.3 Other animals
Rabbit and hare are not kosher (no split hoof)
([Yale](https://chaplain.yale.edu/sites/default/files/2024-10/Jewish%20Dietary%20Restrictions%20Reference%20Sheet.pdf) **[M]**:
"Less common meats that are not permitted include rabbit, hare, camel, any
rodent, reptile, amphibian or insect"). Frog legs follow from
"amphibian". Birds of prey are forbidden but never on a menu.

### 24.4 Blood
Blood is forbidden, so kosher meat is salted to draw it out
([Wikipedia, Kosher foods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kosher_foods) **[M]**):
black pudding, blood pudding, blood sausage, morcilla, boudin noir,
blutwurst (the dishes are culinary knowledge; they are also pork).

### 24.5 Meat with dairy
Meat and dairy may not be eaten together, and the rule extends to
**poultry**; **fish with dairy is permitted**
([Yale](https://chaplain.yale.edu/sites/default/files/2024-10/Jewish%20Dietary%20Restrictions%20Reference%20Sheet.pdf) **[M]**:
"it is acceptable to eat dairy with fish or with eggs";
[Medical News Today, "What is kosher"](https://www.medicalnewstoday.com/articles/what-is-kosher) **[M]**:
"The rabbis extended this prohibition to include not eating milk and
poultry together"; [UKY FCS3-577](https://publications.ca.uky.edu/sites/publications.ca.uky.edu/files/FCS3577.pdf) **[M]**).
No single word names it, so the app reads it as a **rule over two words of
one dish**: a meat or poultry word (alpha-gal's mammal meats and cuts, §17,
plus chicken, turkey, duck, goose, burger, steak, schnitzel, kebab,
shawarma, sausage …) and a stated or likely dairy word (the Milk
vocabulary, §3). Both in the dish → **stated** when both are stated, else
suspected; one only in an option → suspected, asking whether one can be
left off. A guarded word on either side (veggie burger, goat cheese,
coconut milk, "no cheese") never counts, and fish never does, so salmon
in a cream sauce is not flagged. Cheeseburger and cheesesteak name both at
once and are direct triggers. *Parve* cream substitutes are common in
Israeli kosher meat restaurants and a menu rarely says so: a stated pair
is a question for the waiter, not proof.

### 24.6 Gelatin
Mainstream agencies (OK, Star-K, OU) do not accept gelatin from non-kosher
animals; kosher gelatin is from kosher-slaughtered cattle, kosher fish or
agar ([OK Kosher, "A closer look: gelatin"](https://www.ok.org/article/closer-look-gelatin) **[S]**;
[OU Kosher, "Kosher gelatin"](https://oukosher.org/blog/articles/kosher-gelatin-innovation-and-tradition/) **[S]**;
[OK, "Fish gelatin"](https://www.ok.org/article/fish-gelatin/) **[S]**).
A menu never says which, so gelatin and the desserts set with it (panna
cotta, marshmallow, gummies, aspic) are **suspected**; "fish gelatin" is
guarded.

### 24.7 Guards
"No", "without", "-free", "vegan", "plant-based", "meatless",
"imitation", "faux", "mock", "substitute", and **"kosher" beside the
word** ("kosher bacon" is beef bacon; "סרטן כשר" is surimi, file 12 §2.2).
The meat words beside a cured meat ("turkey bacon", "בייקון בקר",
"פרושוטו אווז") and the compounds that name another food ("crab apple",
"oyster mushroom", "eel sauce", "salmon caviar"). A guard lowers to
suspected and never cancels (§0). "Vegetarian" is not a guard, as for the
sea group.

### 24.8 Confusions
- **Certification is not a component.** A dish with no flagged word is
  "No trigger found — ask the restaurant" for a kosher profile, never
  "fits", and the chef card asks "Is your meat kosher-certified?" on its
  own line.
- **A platform's kosher tag** (10bis `kosher: true`) lowers the kosher
  concerns of its dish by one step, as every declaration does (ALG-09); it
  never speaks for halal. "Kosher salt" and "kosher-style" in menu text
  declare nothing.
- "Kosher" in the trip lexicon: an entry that hides shellfish is not
  kosher by definition, so trip mode adds kosher to it.

**Sources (24):** see the inline links; all were read as search-result
snippets on 2026-10-21. **Unreviewed:** the culinary word lists (which
cured meats are usually pork, which desserts hold gelatin) are desk
research, not sourced word by word.

> 2026-10-21, not reviewed by a scholar. **Components only, never
> certification**: whether meat is *zabiha* (slaughtered as halal) is the
> restaurant's to show, and the chef card asks "Is your meat
> halal-certified?".

**Regulated:** nowhere as an allergen.

### 25.1 Pork
Pork and its by-products are always haram, with lard a common cooking fat
([Yale Chaplain's Office, Muslim dietary restrictions](https://chaplain.yale.edu/muslim-dietary-restrictions) **[M]**;
[Maine DOE, Halal foods fact sheet](https://www.maine.gov/doe/sites/maine.gov.doe/files/inline-files/Halal%20Foods%20Fact%20Sheet.pdf) **[S]**;
[City of Toronto, Halal fact sheet](https://www.toronto.ca/wp-content/uploads/2023/08/857f-HalalFactSheet20235.pdf) **[S]**).
The pork lists are §24.1's, shared.

### 25.2 Alcohol
Intoxicating drinks are haram, **in cooking as well as in a glass**: wine,
beer, liquor, cooking wine and mirin are named
([Maine DOE](https://www.maine.gov/doe/sites/maine.gov.doe/files/inline-files/Halal%20Foods%20Fact%20Sheet.pdf) **[S]**;
[Halal Foundation, halal and haram ingredients](https://halalfoundation.org/halal-and-haram-ingredients-list/) **[M]**;
[Institute of Science Tokyo, halal guide](https://discover.titech.ac.jp/2025/08/life-as-a-muslim-in-tokyo-an-introductory-guide-to-eating-halal/) **[M]**,
which also flags rice wine in Japanese rice dishes and soups). Some
opinions differ on traces (brewer's yeast, Yale), which the app does not
read. Every alcohol word is **hidden and likely** — the menu names the
drink, not its alcohol, and some kitchens cook it off: wine, beer, lager,
rum, vodka, whisky, bourbon, brandy, cognac, liqueur, sake, mirin, marsala,
sherry, gin, tequila, champagne, prosecco, arak, ouzo, sangria, cocktail,
flambé, beurre blanc, coq au vin, bourguignon, limoncello, amaretto,
mojito, martini, vermouth, Irish coffee, zabaglione; suspected:
tiramisu, risotto, teriyaki, fondue. The dish list is culinary knowledge.

### 25.3 Blood
Flowing blood is forbidden; halal slaughter drains it
([Toronto fact sheet](https://www.toronto.ca/wp-content/uploads/2023/08/857f-HalalFactSheet20235.pdf) **[S]**).
The blood dishes are §24.4's.

### 25.4 Gelatin
Gelatin is haram unless from fish or a halal source, and is to be avoided
unless labelled halal or kosher
([Maine DOE](https://www.maine.gov/doe/sites/maine.gov.doe/files/inline-files/Halal%20Foods%20Fact%20Sheet.pdf) **[S]**;
[Halal Foundation](https://halalfoundation.org/halal-and-haram-ingredients-list/) **[M]**):
suspected, as in §24.6.

### 25.5 Guards
As §24.7 with "halal" for "kosher", plus the words that mark a drink
without alcohol ("non-alcoholic", "alcohol-free", "ללא אלכוהול", "נטול
אלכוהול") and the compounds that are not alcohol: **wine vinegar** (vinegar
is generally permitted; unreviewed for every school), root and ginger
beer, malt beer (בירה שחורה), a shrimp cocktail or cocktail sauce, and
"sake" before nigiri, maki or sashimi, where it is the Japanese word for
salmon.

### 25.6 Confusions
- **Shellfish is not flagged for halal.** The Shafi'i, Maliki and Hanbali
  schools treat all seafood as halal; the Hanafi school limits it to fish
  and disputes shrimp
  ([Wikipedia, Islamic dietary laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_dietary_laws) **[M]**;
  [ISA Halal, "Can Muslims eat shrimp"](https://www.isahalal.com/news-events/blog/halal-seafood-can-muslims-eat-shrimp-and-other-shellfish) **[M]**;
  [SeekersGuidance, squid in the Hanafi school](https://seekersguidance.org/answers/hanafi-fiqh/is-it-permissible-to-eat-squid-in-the-hanafi-school/) **[M]**).
  A Hanafi diner adds the Crustaceans and Molluscs entries, or their own
  words.
- **Meat that is not zabiha** cannot be read from a menu: it is the
  certificate question.
- A kosher tag never lowers halal: the two rules differ (alcohol, slaughter).

## 26. Buckwheat and the foods on Japan's recommended list (issue #342; desk research, brief)

> **Status: desk research, not clinical review.** Written on 2026-10-21
> for the `vocab_foods_extra.dart` follow-up of issue #342 and **checked
> 2026-10-21 against web search results only**: as everywhere in this
> file, no page could be opened in full, so each claim rests on the result
> excerpt of the URL beside it, graded as in file 12 (**[S]** strong,
> **[M]** medium, **[W]** weak). Rows marked **unreviewed** have no
> source beyond general culinary knowledge; their vocabulary terms are
> `suspected` or left out. No allergist or dietitian has read this
> section. (§24–§25 are the kosher and halal sections.)

**Regulated:** buckwheat is one of Japan's nine *mandatory* labelling
items; kiwi, peach, apple, banana, orange, beef, pork, chicken, gelatin
and yam are among its twenty *recommended* ones (file 02; the list is
confirmed by a Tokyo Metropolitan Government handout, which names
"Yams" and "Gelatin" in English,
https://www.hokeniryo1.metro.tokyo.lg.jp/shokuhin/hyouji/kyouzai/files/nihongo(r8.4)_tenpu.pdf
**[S]**, and by Kyoto Prefecture's English page,
https://www.pref.kyoto.jp/en/labelling_of_food_with_allergens.html
**[S]**). None is on the US, EU or Israeli list, and Japan's rules bind
packaged food only (file 12 §8.1). Every term in this section is
therefore a menu word for a person who has *chosen* the restriction; the
app says "ask the restaurant", never "safe".

### 26.1 Buckwheat

**Direct:** buckwheat, kasha (the Eastern European name for buckwheat
groats), soba (buckwheat noodles), *sarrasin* and *blé noir* (French),
*grano saraceno* (Italian), *juwari* soba (100% buckwheat). **Hidden:**
pizzoccheri (Valtellina buckwheat pasta) and polenta taragna (likely);
galettes, crêpes and blinis, which are buckwheat pancakes in some regions
and wheat in others, and poffertjes (suspected); gluten-free breads and
flours, because buckwheat is a common gluten-free flour (suspected);
buckwheat honey (suspected: a honey, not the grain — **unreviewed**).
Sources: Anaphylaxis UK, buckwheat factsheet ("grano saraceno", "blé
noir", galettes, crêpes, blinis, pizzoccheri, polenta taragna,
poffertjes, caution on gluten-free food)
https://www.anaphylaxis.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/Buckwheat-V5-neffy-update-25.pdf
**[S]** ; Heffler et al., "Buckwheat allergy: an emerging clinical
problem in Europe" (anaphylaxis from a pizza dough of mixed wheat and
buckwheat flour)
https://iris.unito.it/retrieve/handle/2318/1645935/354564/buckwheat-allergy-an-emerging-clinical-problem-in-europe-2155-6121.1000168.pdf
**[S]** ; soba's buckwheat-and-wheat mix, file 12 §8.2.
**Guards:** "no buckwheat", "buckwheat-free", "ללא כוסמת".
**Confusions:**
- **Buckwheat is not wheat** (§7.5): it stays a *guard* of the wheat and
  gluten triggers ("buckwheat flour" lowers wheat's "flour"), and that
  guard never lowers a buckwheat hit.
- **Yakisoba is not soba**: wheat noodles despite the name (file 12
  §8.2); "yakisoba" is one word to the matcher and never matches "soba",
  and "yaki soba" written apart is lowered.
- Soba may be mostly wheat (file 12 §8.2): a soba dish is buckwheat *and*
  wheat, never one instead of the other.
- In Hebrew, כוסמת is buckwheat and כוסמין is spelt (file 12 §10.1).

### 26.2 Kiwi

**Direct:** kiwi, kiwifruit. **Hidden (suspected):** fruit salads, fruit
mixes and smoothies, desserts and tropical juices (Thermo Fisher's kiwi
allergen sheet lists syrups, shakes, cocktails, puddings and fruit sauces;
a consumer page names fruit mixes, smoothies, desserts and salads):
https://www.thermofisher.com/allergy/us/en/allergen-fact-sheets/kiwi.html
**[S]** ; https://www.wyndly.com/blogs/learn/kiwi-allergy **[W]**.
Pavlova, often topped with kiwi, is **unreviewed** (suspected). Some
people tolerate heated kiwi, others not (Thermo Fisher), so a cooked dish
is no guard. Kiwi used as a meat tenderiser was **not confirmed** by any
source and is left out. Kiwi belongs to the latex-fruit syndrome with
banana and avocado (§26.5).

### 26.3 Peach

**Direct:** peach. **Hidden:** nectarine (**likely**): the nectarine is a
smooth-skinned form of the same species, *Prunus persica*, and its fruit
carries the same lipid transfer protein, Pru p 3, the main peach allergen
(Botton et al., Pru p 3 in every peach *and* nectarine cultivar studied
but one: https://research.unipd.it/handle/11577/2481986 **[S]** ;
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8569022 **[S]**). Pru p 3 resists
heat and is concentrated in the skin
(https://www.altmeyers.org/en/allergology/peach-allergy-133292 **[M]**),
so a cooked or peeled peach is no guard. A Bellini (peach purée and
sparkling wine) is **unreviewed** (suspected).

### 26.4 Apple

**Direct:** apple, apples, applesauce. **Hidden (suspected):** cider,
calvados (apple brandy), tarte Tatin and Waldorf salad — **unreviewed**:
no source reviewed names them, but each is made from apple by definition.
Apple allergy has two forms: a birch-pollen form (Mal d 1, destroyed by
cooking, so many people tolerate cooked apple) and a lipid-transfer form
(Mal d 3, heat-stable), and an apple drink has caused anaphylaxis
(https://www.altmeyers.org/en/allergology/apple-allergy-133032 **[M]** ;
https://www.thermofisher.com/phadia/us/en/resources/allergen-encyclopedia/f49.html
**[S]**), so cooking is never a guard. **Guards:** "pineapple" is one word
and never matches; custard, sugar, star, rose and wax apples are other
fruits (lowered — **unreviewed**). In Hebrew תפוח אדמה is potato, not
apple (file 12 §10.4).

### 26.5 Banana

**Direct:** banana, bananas. **Hidden:** banoffee (banana and toffee,
likely), smoothies and banana bread follow from "banana" (suspected:
"banana flavoring in baked goods or smoothies",
https://www.healthline.com/health/banana-allergy **[M]**). Plantain is a
close relative, but **no source reviewed** says a banana-allergic person
reacts to it: suspected. Banana is the commonest fruit of the latex-fruit
syndrome (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11278189/ **[S]**).
**Guards:** a banana pepper is a chilli and a banana shallot a shallot; a
banana leaf is a wrapper (lowered, not cleared: the leaf touches the
food) — **unreviewed**.

### 26.6 Orange

**Direct:** orange, oranges (orange juice, zest and peel follow). **Hidden
(suspected):** marmalade (orange marmalade is the classic; other citrus
exist), and the orange liqueurs Grand Marnier, Cointreau, triple sec and
curaçao: orange extracts and peel oils are used "in the liqueur and food
industry" and peel-oil components are possible allergens
(https://www.altmeyers.org/en/allergology/orange-133441 **[M]**); the
liqueurs by name are **unreviewed**. Germin-like allergens are shared
across the peels of orange, lemon and mandarin
(https://mdpi-res.com/d_attachment/cimb/cimb-47-00621/article_deploy/cimb-47-00621.pdf
**[S]**), but Japan's item is orange alone, so mandarin, lemon and the
other citrus are left out. **Guards (real false positives on menus):**
orange roughy is a deep-sea fish, *Hoplostethus atlanticus*
(https://www.fishbase.org/summary/334 **[S]**), and orange wine is a
skin-contact white wine that holds no orange
(https://www.wine.com/content/article/orange-wine-guide **[M]**). "Orange"
as a colour (orange peppers, orange lentils) was **not** shown by a source
to be a real menu false positive and is not guarded.

### 26.7 Beef

**Direct:** beef, veal ("the meat from a calf or young beef animal", USDA
FSIS, https://www.fsis.usda.gov/es/node/3531 **[S]**), named beef cuts
(ribeye, sirloin, entrecôte, filet mignon, brisket, oxtail, osso buco)
and breeds (wagyu, Angus), bresaola (air-dried salted beef,
https://www.yourdictionary.com/bresaola **[W]**), corned beef and roast
beef. **Hidden:** steak, burgers, carpaccio and pastrami (cured, spiced,
smoked beef in the US,
https://www.mentalfloss.com/food/pastrami-vs-corned-beef-what-is-difference
**[M]**), Bolognese and ragù (likely; turkey, chicken, tuna and plant
versions are guards); meatballs, kebab, kofta, gyro, "meat", stock,
broth, gravy, demi-glace, bone marrow, tallow and gelatin (suspected).
The main beef allergen, bovine serum albumin (Bos d 6), is also in cow's
milk, and some beef-allergic people react to milk, veal and beef
by-products; a consumer page advises reading for beef broth, beef stock,
bovine gelatin and beef flavouring
(https://thermofisher.com/phadia/gb/en/resources/allergen-encyclopedia/f27.html
**[S]** ; https://riteaid.com/health/allergic-reactions-to-beef **[W]**).
Milk is **not** listed here: the Milk restriction carries it, as for
alpha-gal (§17.5). Beef allergy (a protein allergy) is not alpha-gal
syndrome (a sugar), which also covers pork and lamb (§17).

### 26.8 Pork

**Direct:** pork, pig, suckling pig, wild boar, bacon, ham, gammon, lard,
lardo, lardons, pancetta, guanciale (cured pork jowl,
https://www.wikipedia.com/wiki/Guanciale **[W]**), prosciutto, jamón,
porchetta, carnitas, char siu (Chinese barbecued pork), tonkatsu
(breaded fried pork cutlet, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tonkatsu
**[W]**) and tonkotsu (pork-bone broth). **Hidden:** chorizo, mortadella,
speck, bratwurst, kielbasa, chicharrón and spare ribs (likely); salami,
pepperoni, sausages, hot dogs, carbonara and amatriciana (guanciale or
pancetta in the classic recipe) and gelatin (suspected). Consumer
guidance names ham, bacon, sausage, pork-based broths and gravies,
gelatin from pork collagen, lard and bacon drippings
(https://riteaid.com/health/pork-allergy **[W]** ;
https://livestrong.com/article/330965-what-foods-to-avoid-if-allergic-to-pork
**[W]**). Pork allergy is often the pork–cat syndrome, a cross-reaction
of cat and pig serum albumin (https://dx.doi.org/10.20344/amp.15483
**[S]** ; https://departments.med.virginia.edu/medannual2013/?p=1145
**[M]**). **Guards:** turkey, beef, duck, goose and chicken bacon, ham,
salami and sausages (kosher menus print them often); a guinea pig is not
a pig dish. The kosher and halal follow-up (`vocab_religious.dart`, §24–
§25) builds a pork list of its own; the two may later share one.

### 26.9 Chicken

**Direct:** chicken, poussin, capon, Cornish hen, coq au vin, *pollo*.
**Hidden:** wings, nuggets and tenders (likely: chicken unless the menu
says otherwise); schnitzel (suspected in English; Israel's is "almost
always" poultry, chicken or turkey,
https://myjewishlearning.com/the-nosher/how-schnitzel-became-an-israeli-staple
**[M]** ; https://forward.com/food/132482/foods-of-israel-schnitzel/
**[M]**); shawarma (suspected: in Israel turkey is the most popular,
chicken common,
https://www.tastingtable.com/1150931/the-untraditional-meat-thats-popular-for-israeli-shawarma
**[M]**); poultry, stock, broth and bouillon (suspected: a clinician "may
advise avoiding chicken broth",
https://www.medicalnewstoday.com/articles/323070 **[M]**; chicken
bouillon is made from chicken bones and meat). **Guards (real false
positives):** chicken-fried steak is beef fried like chicken; chicken of
the woods is a mushroom; chicken eggs are eggs, not chicken meat (the
bird–egg syndrome links the two in some people, but the Eggs restriction
carries eggs) — **unreviewed** desk knowledge.

### 26.10 Gelatin

**Direct:** gelatin, gelatine, aspic, Jell-O. **Hidden:** gummy sweets,
marshmallows and panna cotta (likely: gummies and marshmallows are
"particularly problematic", https://alphagalinformation.org/gelatin
**[M]**; panna cotta and marshmallow, §17.2); jelly, mousse, no-bake
cheesecake, trifle, bavarois, terrine and mirror glaze (suspected: set with gelatin in
many recipes — **unreviewed**). Gelatin comes from pig skin, cattle hide
and bone, or fish; a fish-gelatin marshmallow has caused anaphylaxis
(https://www.ovid.com/journals/jaci/pdf/00004483-200903000-00036~anaphylaxis-provoked-by-ingestion-of-marshmallows-containing
**[S]**), so "fish gelatin" stays a gelatin trigger. Some yogurts hold
gelatin (https://www.wyndly.com/blogs/learn/gelatin-allergy **[W]**), but a
restaurant's yogurt sauce rarely does, and listing yogurt would flood
every labneh and tzatziki: left out. **Guards:** agar, vegan and
"gelatin-free" lower a hit.

### 26.11 Yam

**What it is:** Japan's *yamaimo* (やまいも), the Japanese mountain yams
of the genus *Dioscorea*: *nagaimo*, *yamaimo*, *jinenjo*; *tororo* is
the grated yam (https://www.masterclass.com/articles/tororo-explained
**[M]**; the Tokyo and Kyoto lists above). **It is not sweet potato**:
true yams (Dioscoreaceae) and sweet potatoes (morning-glory family) are
not related, and what a US menu calls "yams" is almost always a sweet
potato (Library of Congress,
https://www.loc.gov/everyday-mysteries/agriculture/item/what-is-the-difference-between-sweet-potatoes-and-yams/
**[S]**). So the bare word "yam" is **not** a trigger (candied yams are
sweet potato; Thai *yam* is a salad — **unreviewed**), and בטטה is never
one. **Direct:** yamaimo, nagaimo, jinenjo, tororo, Japanese, Chinese and
mountain yam. **Hidden:** yamakake (tuna or soba under tororo, likely);
okonomiyaki, whose batter often holds grated nagaimo, though some recipes
leave it out (suspected,
https://www.americastestkitchen.com/recipes/16992-japanese-yam-okonomiyaki
**[M]** ; https://www.justonecookbook.com/wprm_print/okonomiyaki
**[W]**); hanpen fish cake, some of which list yamaimo (suspected,
https://greenbeans.com/products/14971661153141/details **[W]**); purple
yam and ube (suspected — **unreviewed** whether Japan's item covers
*D. alata*).
